# The walls and gates of Florence

Florence was enclosed six times. Each new circle took in the suburbs that had grown outside the last one. The final
circle, begun in 1284, stood almost whole until the 1860s. Its gates still stand along the *viali*, and long stretches of
it still climb the hills of Oltrarno.

## The circles

- **Roman Florentia** (1st century BC). The colony's castrum was a rectangle of about 500 × 400 m, centred on the forum,
  today's Piazza della Repubblica, with round towers and four gates. Its outline still shows in the street grid between
  Via Tornabuoni, Via del Proconsolo, the Duomo and Via Porta Rossa.
- **Byzantine and Carolingian walls** (6th–9th c.). A smaller ring after the Gothic wars, then rebuilt and enlarged again
  under the Carolingians.
- **The Matildine circle** (1078). Built in the time of Countess Matilda of Canossa, it reached the Arno on the south side.
- **The first communal circle** (1172–75). The free commune walled in the suburbs on both banks, including Oltrarno.
  The Oltrarno part was extended around 1258–60.
- **The sixth, Arnolfian circle** (1284–1333). Planned under Arnolfo di Cambio, it was about 8.5 km long with 73 towers and
  15 gates. According to Giovanni Villani, the walls were 20 braccia high (about 11.6 m) and the towers 40 braccia (about
  23 m), one every 200 braccia (about 115 m). The circle was so generous that its fields and orchards were still being
  filled with houses in the 19th century.

## Through time

| Year | What happened |
|---|---|
| 1284 | Work begins on the new circle on the north bank. Porta alla Croce and Porta al Prato are among the first gates. |
| 1285 | Porta San Gallo is begun. Its inscription names the year 1284 and Rolandino da Canossa. |
| 1320–1334 | Oltrarno: Porta San Miniato (1320), San Niccolò and San Giorgio (1324), Porta Romana (1326–28), and Porta San Frediano (1332–34, attributed to Andrea Pisano, never finished in height). |
| 1333 | The circle is closed. |
| 1526–1530 | Under the threat of artillery, and during the siege of 1529–30, the towers and gates are cut down to about the height of the walls. Earth ramparts and bastions are thrown up. Michelangelo designs the defences of San Miniato. Torre San Niccolò is spared. |
| 1534–37 | Alessandro de' Medici builds the Fortezza da Basso over the walls by Porta a Faenza, a fortress that watches the city as much as it guards it. |
| 1590 | The Forte di Belvedere is built on the hill above Boboli. |
| 1739 | The Arco di Trionfo is raised outside Porta San Gallo for the entry of Francis Stephen of Lorraine (Jean-Nicolas Jadot, 1739–45). |
| 1865–1870 | Florence becomes capital of Italy. Giuseppe Poggi's plan pulls down the north-bank walls and replaces them with the *viali*. The gates are left standing alone in new squares: Piazza Beccaria, Piazza della Libertà and Porta al Prato. Porta a Pinti is demolished. On the south bank the walls stay. |
| 1872–76 | Piazza Poggi and the ramps below Piazzale Michelangelo take the place of the stretch from San Niccolò to the river. |
| 2014–16 | The Torre della Zecca is restored and opened to visitors. The towers and gates are opened for summer visits. |

## The gates and towers today

- **Porta Romana**, on the road to Siena and Rome. It is the second-largest gate. Its huge wooden doors are the originals,
  and there is a fresco of the Madonna in the lunette on the city side.
- **Porta San Frediano**, on the road to Pisa. It is the largest gate and was never finished in height. It keeps its
  14th-century doors with their nails and hinges, and a marble lily on the city side.
- **Porta alla Croce**, in Piazza Beccaria. It was cut down in the siege and stands alone in the middle of the square.
- **Porta al Prato**, named after the *prato*, the open field used as a cattle market.
- **Porta San Gallo**, in Piazza della Libertà, beside the Arco di Trionfo dei Lorena.
- **Torre San Niccolò**, the only tower not cut down. Like all the gate towers, it is open towards the city, and its floors
  and stairs can be seen from the piazza.
- **Porta San Giorgio**, on the hill, has a relief of St George outside (the original is in Palazzo Vecchio) and Bicci di
  Lorenzo's Madonna inside. **Porta San Miniato** is a plain arch in the wall, with no tower.
- **Torre della Zecca**, the end tower on the north bank by the river, next to the old mint. **Torre della Serpe** is by
  the Fortezza, and the **Torrino di Santa Rosa** stands on the river west of San Frediano.

## In the app

From 1284 the walls stand along their mapped course, with their towers. In 1529 the gates and towers are cut down,
in 1866–68 the north bank disappears, and today's walls are measured from the LiDAR survey. Each gate has its own card.
